---
title: Science Cases
description: Astrophysical motivation for a real-time, all-sky, high-time-resolution imager below 100 MHz.
---

Each EPIC image at LWA-SV covers approximately $2\pi$ sr, the entire sky above the horizon. One image is formed for every 25 kHz channel at the 40 µs sampling interval of the station F-engine. This combination of field of view and time resolution is not available from beamformed observations, which sample a small number of directions, or from FX imaging, which is constrained to integration times of seconds by visibility data rates. The science cases below motivated the design of the system (Thyagarajan et al. 2017; Kent et al. 2019; Krishnan et al. 2023; Reddy et al. 2024).

## Fast radio bursts

Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are extragalactic radio transients of millisecond duration. Propagation through ionized media introduces a frequency-dependent delay:

$$
\Delta t \simeq 4.15\ \text{ms}\;\; \mathrm{DM}\left[\left(\frac{\nu_1}{\text{GHz}}\right)^{-2} - \left(\frac{\nu_2}{\text{GHz}}\right)^{-2}\right],
$$

where the dispersion measure DM is expressed in pc cm⁻³.

Below 100 MHz this delay amounts to hundreds of seconds for typical FRB dispersion measures. A detection at higher frequency, for example by CHIME/FRB at 400–800 MHz, therefore precedes the arrival of the burst in the LWA band by several minutes. EPIC supports two complementary modes:

- **Blind search.** All directions are imaged simultaneously, so a search of the full sky requires no pointing.
- **Triggered follow-up.** External alerts, distributed as VOEvents, are added to the watch list. Pixels at the alert position are then recorded around the predicted arrival time.

Detection at these frequencies is limited by scatter broadening, which scales approximately as $\nu^{-4}$. A burst broadened by 1 ms at 1.4 GHz would be broadened by several minutes at 50 MHz ([Memo 006](https://github.com/epic-astronomy/Memos/blob/master/PDFs/006_Sensitivity_Dispersion_curves.pdf)). Non-detections therefore constrain the scattering environment and the low-frequency spectra of FRBs. A search of database spectrograms for CHIME-triggered events is reported in [Memo 013](https://github.com/epic-astronomy/Memos/blob/master/PDFs/013_searchForFRBS.pdf).

## Solar radio emission

The Sun is the brightest variable source in the low-frequency sky. Its main types of radio emission are:

- **Type III bursts**, produced by electron beams propagating through the corona.
- **Type II bursts**, associated with shocks driven by coronal mass ejections.
- **Fine structure** in both burst types, which evolves on timescales of tens of milliseconds.

Krishnan et al. (2023) showed that EPIC dynamic spectra at 81.92 ms and 25 kHz resolution reproduce simultaneous beamformed spectra of faint Type III bursts on a one-to-one basis. The Sun is monitored continuously while above the elevation limit.

## Pulsars and giant pulses

Pulsars provide sources of known period and dispersion measure, against which timing and sensitivity can be verified. Because all directions are imaged simultaneously, several pulsars can be folded from a single observation. Krishnan et al. (2023) obtained folded profiles of PSR B0809+74 and PSR B0834+06 from the same session at 43 MHz, and detected a dispersed giant pulse from PSR B1133+16. Searches for Crab giant pulses are described in [Memo 002](https://github.com/epic-astronomy/Memos/blob/master/PDFs/002_Searching_for_CGP.pdf).

## Jovian decametric emission

Jupiter produces intense, strongly circularly polarized emission below about 40 MHz, in both Io-related and non-Io components, with millisecond S-burst structure. The imager records the full set of polarization products. Stokes V is therefore available, and Jupiter is a standing entry in the watch list.

## Ionospheric and interplanetary scintillation

Compact sources such as Cygnus A and Cassiopeia A scintillate on timescales of seconds as their radiation traverses the ionosphere. Because EPIC records every bright source simultaneously, ionospheric structure can be probed along many lines of sight at once. Krishnan et al. (2023) demonstrated ionospheric scintillation of both sources. They also identified interplanetary scintillation, including multi-station observations, as a means of studying the solar wind.

## Meteors and reflected transmissions

The first real-time EPIC observations at LWA-SV recorded a meteor trail reflecting a distant 55.25 MHz analog television transmitter. The event lasted approximately 2 s and was imaged at 50 ms cadence (Kent et al. 2019). Such reflections are of interest for studies of meteor plasma. They are also a class of signal that any transient search at these frequencies must identify and classify.

## Multi-messenger follow-up

The watch list is maintained by a separate service that accepts external alerts. The intended trigger sources include:

- CHIME/FRB
- DSA-110
- realfast at the VLA
- gravitational-wave detections by LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA (Reddy et al. 2024)

Low-frequency dispersion delays are of order minutes. Any prompt coherent radio emission associated with a compact-object merger could therefore be recorded after receipt of the alert.

## Limitations

The real-time system described in this documentation lacks four capabilities:

- **Gain calibration.** It does not perform antenna-based gain calibration. Only the cable delay and gain models of the station are applied.
- **RFI flagging.** It does not flag radio-frequency interference online.
- **Dedispersion.** It does not perform online dedispersion.
- **Transient detection.** It does not perform automated transient detection.

Scientific analyses are therefore carried out on the stored pixel spectrograms and accumulated images. These limitations are discussed further in [Calibration](/docs/theory/calibration) and [Memos](/docs/references/memos).
