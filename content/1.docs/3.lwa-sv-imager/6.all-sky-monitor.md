---
title: All-Sky Monitor
description: Real-time search of every EPIC all-sky image for dispersed radio bursts, with sky-fixed pixel tracking, hierarchical dedispersion, test injections and per-band independence.
---

The EPIC imager forms an image of the whole sky above the horizon every 83.6 ms in each of its 128 channels. The all-sky monitor (`skymon`, in `src/python/skymon` of the LWA_EPIC repository) searches these images for dispersed radio bursts at every position above an elevation of 10° in real time.

Each imaging node runs its own monitor for its own band of 3.06 MHz. A monitor depends on no other node, on neither the database nor the shared file system for its search, and on no other monitor. The loss of a node therefore removes only that band.

## Data path

| Stage | Operation | Implementation |
|---|---|---|
| Publication | Stokes I (XX + YY) of every image, at full channel resolution, written to a ring of slots in shared memory | `epic++ --sky_shm`, `src/ex/sky_publisher.hpp` |
| Tracking | Images interpolated onto a fixed equatorial HEALPix grid | `geometry.py` |
| Normalisation | Running mean and variance per pixel and channel; median over the sky subtracted per channel | `_kernels.c`, `search.py` |
| Dedispersion | Fast dispersion measure transform in octave tiers of time resolution | `dedisp.py`, `_kernels.c` |
| Detection | Boxcar search, clustering, RFI classification | `search.py`, `monitor.py` |
| Products | Candidate records and data, test-injection log, pixel spectrograms | `candidates.py`, `accumulate.py`, `server.py` |

The imager writes each image into the next slot of the ring and never waits for a reader. A monitor that falls behind loses the oldest images and records the loss; a restart of either program does not affect the other. The pixels of the published images are in the order of the saved FITS images, so the monitor uses the imager's celestial coordinate system (`epic_geometry.apply_wcs`) unchanged.

## Sky-fixed pixels

A source drifts across the image by one pixel every few minutes. A dispersed burst at low frequency can take longer than that to cross the band. The time series of a fixed image pixel would therefore follow different parts of the sky over the duration of one burst.

The monitor instead follows HEALPix pixels (J2000, RING order). The tracked set is recomputed every 10 s:

- a pixel that rises above the elevation limit is assigned a slot in the state arrays;
- the slot of a pixel that sets is cleared and returned to the pool.

Between updates, the position of each tracked pixel on the image is interpolated linearly in time, so its sample of the sky moves continuously with the rotation of the Earth. Each image is interpolated at these positions by bicubic convolution (Keys 1981). With about three image pixels across the beam, bilinear interpolation would miss the peak of a source by up to 6% depending on its position between pixels, and the error would ripple at the same time in every channel as the sky turns; bicubic interpolation reduces it to about 2%.

The HEALPix resolution is the coarsest at which a pixel is no larger than half of the synthesised beam $\lambda / D$ ($D \approx 100$ m), and never finer than the image pixel. This gives:

- $N_\mathrm{side} = 64$ ($0.92°$) above 40 MHz, about 20 300 pixels tracked at once;
- $N_\mathrm{side} = 32$ near 35 MHz;
- $N_\mathrm{side} = 16$ at 16–21 MHz.

## Normalisation and excision of interference

The value of each pixel and channel is normalised by a running mean with a time constant of 2 min and a running variance with a time constant of 5 min. The variance is estimated as half the mean square difference between consecutive images, which a slow change of brightness, such as that of a source rising through the primary beam, does not inflate. Deviations beyond five standard deviations are limited before they enter these statistics, so that interference and bursts do not raise the noise estimate.

For each channel and image, the median over the tracked sky is then subtracted. Interference received through the sidelobes and changes of receiver gain affect the whole image, whereas a celestial burst occupies one beam, so the median removes the former and leaves the latter unchanged. Samples beyond 20 standard deviations are set to zero.

## Hierarchical dedispersion

The dispersion delay across the band between $f_\mathrm{lo}$ and $f_\mathrm{hi}$ is

$$
\Delta t = k_\mathrm{DM}\,\mathrm{DM}\left(f_\mathrm{lo}^{-2} - f_\mathrm{hi}^{-2}\right), \qquad k_\mathrm{DM} = 4.1488 \times 10^{3}\ \mathrm{s\ MHz^2\ pc^{-1}\ cm^3}.
$$

At 57 MHz the delay is 0.135 s per unit of DM, so a burst at DM 3000 takes 6.7 min to cross the band. At 16 MHz the same burst takes 5.2 h.

The smearing within a channel equals the sweep across the band divided by the number of channels $N_c$. The search exploits this in tiers:

- Tier $j$ averages $2^j$ images.
- Each tier computes the fast dispersion measure transform (FDMT; Zackay & Ofek 2017) over delays of $0$ to $D = N_c$ samples at its own resolution.
- Because the delay is proportional to DM, every tier uses the same delay table, and tier $j$ covers DMs $2^j$ times those of tier 0.
- A tier above tier 0 reports only delays above $D/2$, so the tiers cover the DM range in octaves.
- At the upper end of each tier, a pulse spans about one sample per channel.

The history a tier needs is $D$ samples plus the block being searched, independent of DM. For 128 channels the search state of all tiers takes about 0.7 MB per pixel, or 14 GB for the whole sky at $N_\mathrm{side} = 64$; the 6-hour spectrogram accumulation takes a further 12 GB. Storing the full-resolution series instead would take 222 000 samples per channel at 16 MHz and DM 3000, or about 1 TB for the sky.

Whether a burst can be detected at a given DM depends on how long its position stays above the elevation limit. A burst whose sweep ends after its position has set is not completed. Above 30 MHz nearly every position in view remains up long enough for DM 3000. At 16–21 MHz, positions near the western horizon limit the reach to a few hundred.

## Detection and classification

Before each transform, a straight line fitted over the window is subtracted from every channel of every pixel, which removes the remaining slow trends; a burst, which occupies about one sample per channel, loses about $1/W$ of its signal for a window of $W$ samples. Above tier 0 the mean over channels at each time is also subtracted (a zero-DM filter; Eatough et al. 2009). This removes any change common to all channels, such as a gain variation or the residual interpolation ripple of a bright source, while a burst in these tiers occupies one or two channels per sample and loses one or two parts in $N_c$. Tier 0 is not filtered in this way, so that bursts and pulsars of low DM keep their full signal. Each tier searches boxcars of 1, 2, 4 and 8 samples at every delay. The noise of every delay and boxcar width is measured from the data themselves, as the clipped root mean square of the previous blocks, so the signal-to-noise ratio does not assume white noise. Values above S/N 8 are retained. They are then:

- clustered over the sky (twice the beam width), over DM and over arrival time;
- each cluster becomes a candidate at its brightest pixel.

A candidate is classified as interference in any of the following cases:

- its cluster extends over more than six beam areas;
- more than 1% of the tracked sky triggers within its time span;
- clusters at four or more well-separated positions coincide with it;
- its DM is below 2 pc cm⁻³.

A burst detected again by another tier or block is stored once, as the detection of highest S/N.

## Data products

Every candidate is written to `<out>/candidates/YYYYMMDD/` as a JSON record. The record holds:

- position and pixel;
- DM, S/N and width;
- arrival times at infinite frequency and at both band edges;
- elevation and azimuth;
- tier;
- the pixels of its cluster.

Every candidate not classified as interference also has an `.npz` file holding:

- the dynamic spectrum of its pixel at the resolution of the tier;
- the DM–time plane of that pixel;
- the accumulated spectrum of the 30 min before the burst;
- the noise table of the search.

The spectrum of every tracked pixel, averaged over 10 s, is retained for 6 h. It serves the [All-sky monitor](/waterfalls/spectrograms/skymon) page of the Waterfalls site, which shows the state of every monitor, the candidates, and the spectrogram of any position in every band that tracks it.

## Test injections

At random times, with a mean interval of one hour by default, a monitor adds a simulated burst to an incoming image before interpolation. The burst has:

- a position that stays above the elevation limit for the whole sweep;
- a DM drawn log-uniformly over the range that position can still complete;
- a target S/N between 10 and 30, set from the measured noise of the pixel.

A candidate that matches an injection is classified as a test injection. Every injection is recorded when it is scheduled and again when it is recovered or missed (`<out>/injections.jsonl`), so the detection efficiency of the running system is measured continuously. The injections exercise every stage after the imager.

## Verification with a simulated sky

The monitor includes a simulator of the sky in the imager's format. It produces images at the imager's rate, with:

- diffuse Galactic emission;
- the four brightest radio sources;
- a primary beam;
- radiometer noise correlated on the scale of the beam;
- whole-sky and narrow-band interference;
- FRBs at random positions, DMs, widths and S/N, recorded in a truth file.

The simulator writes into a shared-memory ring as the imager does, and the monitor reads it unchanged.

The run summarised below covered 30 min of the 55.88–58.94 MHz band. It used:

- 128 channels and 83.6 ms images;
- $N_\mathrm{side} = 64$, with 20 310 pixels tracked;
- DM up to 3000 pc cm⁻³;
- eight threads on cores 24–47 of an imaging node (AMD Threadripper PRO 5965WX);
- 40 FRBs per hour, with DM 20–3000 pc cm⁻³ and target S/N 8–30;
- RFI flashes at 6 per hour;
- a live test injection every 5 min.

## Operation

The monitor runs as the Nomad system job `epic-skymon`, one allocation per imaging node. It runs at reduced priority on processor cores separate from the imager, with eight compute threads, and serves its HTTP interface on port 8095. The imager publishes its images when started with `--sky_shm epic_sky0`; until then the monitor waits for the ring.

```bash
PYTHONPATH=LWA_EPIC/src/python python -m skymon run --shm epic_sky0 --out /FileStore/epic_skymon/epic3 --port 8095 --threads 8
PYTHONPATH=LWA_EPIC/src/python python -m skymon simtest --realtime --duration 1800 --out simtest
```
